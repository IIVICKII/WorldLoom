# WORLDLOOM — GENRE PROFILES

Reference for the Genre Contract that Project 1 writes into every Story Bible (Projects 2 and
3 then read the contract from the bible itself). Genre decides what a story is made of: whether there
is a villain at all, whether characters need flaws and of what weight, how high the stakes go,
how fast pressure builds, and — most important for the critique pass — what "interesting"
means. A cozy story with a menacing villain is not a more interesting cozy story. It is a
different, worse story.

---

## 1. Genre tags versus setting tags

Read the operator's tags in two piles before deriving anything.

- **Setting tags** describe the world: fantasy, sci-fi, historical, modern, academy, royal
  court, post-apocalyptic, isekai world, steampunk. They decide what exists. They never decide
  story shape. "Fantasy" does not imply a dark lord; "royal court" does not imply a coup.
- **Genre tags** describe the story's shape: cozy, slice-of-life, romance, comedy, adventure,
  dark fantasy, intrigue, mystery, thriller, horror, tragedy, sports, survival, action,
  progression, found family, hurt/comfort, drama.

If the operator gave only setting tags, derive the story shape from the premise and dynamics,
choose the gentlest profile the premise actually supports, and say so in Tag Conflict
Resolutions. Never default to an adventure-with-villain shape because the setting is fantastical.

---

## 2. The contract fields

Every Genre Contract states these, each on its own labelled line:

| Field | What it decides |
|---|---|
| Primary genre | The tag that sets the story's shape; the others modify it |
| Stakes scale | intimate · social · local · regional · existential |
| Opposition model | which kinds of opposition this story uses (§3), and the antagonist floor — which may be zero |
| Flaw model | how much flaw characters carry, and whether supporting cast needs any (§4) |
| Pressure tempo | gentle · steady · urgent · relentless |
| Darkness ceiling | what this story will not do on the page |
| Engine | what makes a scene in this genre work — the yardstick for "interesting" |
| Mystery model | only where a central question exists: fixed answer held in reserve, or unresolved-by-design |

---

## 3. Opposition models

Opposition is anything that stands between a character and what they want. Only some of it is
a person, and only some of those people are villains.

| Model | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Friction | Wants that rub against each other between people who like each other | Roommates with incompatible sleep schedules |
| Circumstance | Deadlines, money, rules, distance, weather, bureaucracy | The festival is in three weeks and the oven is broken |
| Internal | A character's own fear, habit, or belief | She will not ask for help, even when it costs her |
| Rival | An opponent with legitimate wants who is not wrong to want them | The other bakery; the rival team's captain; the other suitor |
| Environment | Nature, terrain, scarcity, the world itself | The pass closes with the first snow |
| Hidden culprit | Someone who did something and is concealing it | Whoever moved the ledger |
| Systemic | An institution, faction, or class pressing in | The council that wants the contract reopened |
| Antagonist | An agent who actively wants a Main Cast member to lose, and acts on it | The usurper; the stalker; the thing in the walls |

**A rival is not an antagonist.** A rival can be likeable, can be right, can become a friend.
Writing a rival as a villain is one of the most common genre errors, and it is a bible defect.

**The antagonist floor may be zero.** Many genres run entirely on friction, circumstance, and
internal opposition. Where the floor is zero, Section 4 still exists under its standard title
and states plainly where this story's opposition comes from instead.

---

## 4. Flaw models

A flaw is one source of friction. It is not a required feature of a character.

| Model | What it means | Typical genres |
|---|---|---|
| None required | Defined by want, charm, habit, and function. No flaw needed | Supporting cast in cozy, comedy, slice-of-life; comfort characters; mentors |
| Quirks | Habits that cause friction but not harm | Comedy, cozy, slice-of-life main cast |
| Insecurities | Inner obstacles to closeness, belief, or asking for help | Romance, drama, found family, academy |
| Moral flaws | Choices that hurt other people | Intrigue, thriller, dark fantasy, noir |
| Fatal flaws | The trait that could destroy them or what they love | Tragedy, grimdark |

Rules that hold across every model:

- **Every Main Cast member needs a source of friction** against what they want. That source may
  be a flaw, but it may equally be a circumstance, another person, a limitation, or plain
  inexperience. It does not have to live inside them.
- **Supporting characters need no flaw at all** unless the flaw model calls for one. Give them a
  want, a function, and a texture.
- **A flaw added to satisfy a template reads as a flaw added to satisfy a template.** If a
  character is kind, let them be kind. If their kindness causes friction, that is better than a
  bolted-on defect.
- **Never escalate the flaw model to raise stakes.** A cozy baker does not need a gambling
  problem. An insecurity is enough.

---

## 4a. Cast size is not a genre default

No genre requires supporting characters. Genre sets what kind of opposition a story uses; it does
not set how many people the story needs. Many strong premises run entirely on their Main Cast:

- A two-hander romance, where the two leads are each other's obstacle.
- A survival story with two or three people and a hostile place.
- An enemies-to-lovers or rivals story, where the antagonist floor is met by a lead.
- A chamber piece — one house, one ship, one long night.

Supporting characters and separately written antagonists are invented only when they pass Project
1's Cast Necessity test. Unnamed people — a shopkeeper, a crowd, a guard — supply texture without
becoming cast. Where a genre's antagonist floor is one or more, a Main Cast member who is the
opposition meets it.

## 5. Profiles

Defaults per genre. Blends follow §6.

### Cozy
- Stakes: intimate to local. Opposition: friction, circumstance, internal; rival allowed if
  likeable. **Antagonist floor: 0.** Flaws: quirks (main), none required (supporting).
- Tempo: gentle — weekly, seasonal. Darkness ceiling: no on-page death, no cruelty played
  straight, harm is reversible.
- Escalation looks like: a small thing gets harder, more public, or more urgent — never more
  dangerous.
- Engine: warmth, competence, specificity, small wins, community texture.

### Slice-of-life
- Stakes: intimate. Opposition: friction, circumstance, internal. **Antagonist floor: 0.**
  Flaws: quirks and insecurities.
- Tempo: gentle. Darkness ceiling: ordinary life's sadness is allowed; melodrama is not.
- Escalation: a routine shifts; a relationship changes temperature.
- Engine: everyday moments with emotional truth; noticing.

### Romance (incl. slow burn, rom-com)
- Stakes: intimate. Opposition: internal first, then friction, circumstance, rival suitor.
  **Antagonist floor: 0.** Flaws: insecurities — what each person is afraid wanting will cost.
- Tempo: gentle to steady. Darkness ceiling: set by co-genre.
- Escalation: closeness becomes harder to deny, or harder to reach.
- Engine: obstacles to intimacy that both people contribute to; the push and pull.

### Comedy
- Stakes: social. Opposition: friction, circumstance, rival. **Antagonist floor: 0** (a comic
  foil is a rival). Flaws: quirks, played large.
- Tempo: steady. Darkness ceiling: nothing that cannot be laughed about later.
- Escalation: the misunderstanding compounds, or gets a bigger audience.
- Engine: people wanting incompatible things for good reasons; status reversals.

### Found family
- Stakes: intimate. Opposition: internal, circumstance; outside pressure allowed.
  **Antagonist floor: 0.** Flaws: insecurities, old wounds about belonging.
- Tempo: gentle to steady. Engine: earned trust, small repairs, belonging.

### Hurt/comfort and medical drama
- Stakes: personal, sometimes life-and-death for one person. Opposition: circumstance (the
  illness, the injury), internal (refusing care), systemic allowed. **Antagonist floor: 0.**
  Flaws: insecurities; self-neglect is the classic.
- Tempo: steady. Engine: care, competence under pressure, vulnerability accepted.

### School and academy life
- Stakes: social. Opposition: friction, rival, systemic (ranks, exams), bully allowed.
  **Antagonist floor: 0** — a bully is a rival-scale antagonist at most unless the premise
  says otherwise. Flaws: insecurities, quirks.
- Tempo: steady, on a term calendar. Engine: routine, ranks, cliques, belonging, the next exam.

### Sports and competition
- Stakes: social to local. Opposition: rival, internal, circumstance. **Antagonist floor: 0.**
  Flaws: insecurities, pride.
- Tempo: steady, set by the fixture list. Engine: training, rivalry with respect, team dynamics.

### Adventure and high fantasy
- Stakes: local to existential. Opposition: environment, antagonist, rival.
  **Antagonist floor: 1** unless the premise is exploration-only. Flaws: insecurities, moral
  flaws allowed.
- Tempo: steady to urgent. Engine: a goal, obstacles, discovery, wonder.

### Action and battle
- Stakes: local to regional. Opposition: rival, antagonist. **Antagonist floor: 1.**
  Flaws: pride, recklessness.
- Tempo: urgent. Engine: power, rivalry, challenges worth rising to.

### Progression, LitRPG, isekai
- Stakes: personal to regional. Opposition: environment, rival, systemic; antagonist optional.
  **Antagonist floor: 0.** Flaws: quirks, insecurities.
- Tempo: steady. Engine: rules worth learning, advancement possible, discovery.

### Political intrigue and court drama
- Stakes: social to regional. Opposition: systemic, rival, hidden culprit; antagonist allowed.
  **Antagonist floor: 0** — factions may oppose without anyone being a villain. Flaws: moral
  flaws, ambition.
- Tempo: steady. Engine: information as currency; who knows what, and who owes whom.

### Mystery and detective
- Stakes: personal to local. Opposition: hidden culprit, circumstance. **Antagonist floor: 1**
  (the culprit counts). Flaws: insecurities, obsession.
- Tempo: steady. Engine: fair clues, suspects with motive and opportunity, red herrings.
- **Mystery model: fixed answer held in reserve.** The central question's answer is decided in
  the bible as a Secret known to the culprit, so clues can converge fairly. Peripheral
  questions may still be unresolved-by-design. A whodunit whose answer is unresolved-by-design
  cannot produce clues that add up.

### Thriller and suspense
- Stakes: personal to regional. Opposition: antagonist, systemic. **Antagonist floor: 1.**
  Flaws: moral flaws, paranoia.
- Tempo: urgent to relentless. Engine: time pressure against competent opposition.

### Horror
- Stakes: personal to existential. Opposition: antagonist (monster, entity, killer),
  environment. **Antagonist floor: 1.** Flaws: insecurities, denial.
- Tempo: steady building to relentless. Engine: dread; safety eroding; the unknown.
- Mystery model: the threat's true nature may be unresolved-by-design — the unknown is the point.

### Dark fantasy and grimdark
- Stakes: regional to existential. Opposition: antagonist, systemic, environment.
  **Antagonist floor: 1.** Flaws: moral flaws, fatal flaws.
- Tempo: urgent. Engine: costly choices; nobody clean.

### Tragedy and literary drama
- Stakes: intimate to social. Opposition: internal above all. **Antagonist floor: 0** — the
  flaw is often the antagonist. Flaws: fatal flaws.
- Tempo: steady. Engine: the gap between what someone wants and what their nature makes them do.

### Survival and disaster
- Stakes: personal, life-and-death. Opposition: environment, circumstance, friction under
  strain. **Antagonist floor: 0.** Flaws: insecurities, selfishness under pressure.
- Tempo: urgent. Engine: scarcity; trust under strain.

## 6. Blending tags

Most stories carry two or three genre tags. Blend in this order:

1. **The first-listed genre tag is primary** unless another tag is plainly the story's shape
   and the first is a modifier. Record the choice in Tag Conflict Resolutions.
2. **Tone genres set the ceiling; plot genres supply the engine.** Cozy, slice-of-life,
   comedy, romance, and found family are tone genres. Intrigue, mystery, adventure, thriller,
   and horror are plot genres. When they collide, the plot shrinks to fit the tone — never the
   tone stretched to fit the plot. Cozy intrigue is a warehouse contract, not a coup. Romantic
   mystery is a missing letter, not a serial killer.
3. **Take the lower stakes, the gentler tempo, and the lower antagonist floor** of the tone
   genre, unless the operator's premise explicitly demands more.
4. **Take the opposition models of both**, filtered through the tone genre's darkness ceiling.
   A rival from the plot genre stays a rival in a cozy blend; an antagonist from the plot genre
   becomes a systemic pressure or a rival.
5. **Where two dark genres blend**, take the higher stakes and the faster tempo.
